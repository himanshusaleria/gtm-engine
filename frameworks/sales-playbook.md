# The Founder's Sales Playbook — 7-Stage Deal Checklist

A step-by-step operational playbook for founders who've never run a sales process. From finding a prospect to closing the deal and handing off to your team.

## What Is It?

The Founder's Sales Playbook breaks every deal into seven stages:

```
Research → Outreach → Discovery → Demo → Proof → Close → Handoff
```

Each stage has a clear objective, key activities, exit criteria (what must be true before you advance), and common mistakes. Think of it as a pre-flight checklist for every deal — miss a step and the whole thing can crash.

| Stage | Objective | Typical Duration |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|
| **1. Research** | Know enough to be relevant | 30 min — 2 hours |
| **2. Outreach** | Start a conversation | 1 — 14 days |
| **3. Discovery** | Understand their world and pain | 1 — 2 calls |
| **4. Demo** | Show the solution mapped to their pain | 1 call |
| **5. Proof** | Prove it works in their environment | 1 — 4 weeks |
| **6. Close** | Get the commitment (contract, PO, handshake) | 1 — 3 weeks |
| **7. Handoff** | Transition from sales to delivery | 1 week |

**Total typical cycle for early-stage B2B SaaS:** 4-12 weeks from first outreach to close.

## Why It Matters for 0-1 Sales

Most founders don't have a sales process. They have a vague loop:

```
Talk to someone → Demo the product → Hope they buy → Repeat
```

This fails because:
- You demo before understanding the problem (wasted demo)
- You skip research and send generic outreach (low response rate)
- You never establish proof (prospect can't justify the purchase internally)
- You don't know what "done" looks like at each stage (deals stall mysteriously)
- You don't hand off properly (churn starts before the contract is signed)

A playbook doesn't make sales formulaic — it makes it repeatable. You still need judgment, creativity, and empathy. But you stop losing deals to process failures.

## The Framework

### Stage 1: Research

**Objective:** Know enough about the prospect and their company to be relevant in your outreach and conversations.

**Key Activities:**
- Research the company: what they do, how big they are, recent news, tech stack
- Research the person: role, tenure, background, LinkedIn activity
- Look for buying signals: job postings, tech changes, scaling indicators
- Check your Selling Window assessment: what pain stage is this org at?
- Review your ICP fit: is this company even in your target market?

**Exit Criteria:**
- [ ] You can explain what the company does in one sentence
- [ ] You know who you're reaching out to and why they'd care
- [ ] You've identified at least one relevant pain signal
- [ ] The company passes your ICP screening (Core or Broader tier)

**Common Mistakes:**
- Spending zero time on research and sending a generic "Hi, we do X" message
- Spending 3 hours researching and never actually reaching out (analysis paralysis)
- Researching the company but not the person — they have different pains than the org

**Tools and Skills That Help:**
- `/validate-lead` for ICP scoring
- [Intent Signals](intent-signals.md) for identifying buying signals
- [Customer Profile](customer-profile.md) for ICP fit assessment
- [Selling Window](selling-window.md) for org pain stage

### Stage 2: Outreach

**Objective:** Start a conversation. Not close a deal — just get a reply.

**Key Activities:**
- Write a personalized outreach message (email, LinkedIn, or warm intro)
- Lead with their pain or a relevant signal — not your product
- Keep it short: 4-6 sentences for cold email
- Include a soft CTA: "Worth a 15-min chat?" not "Book a demo with us"
- Plan your follow-up sequence: Day 0, Day 3, Day 7, Day 14

**Exit Criteria:**
- [ ] Prospect has replied (positively or with questions)
- [ ] A call or meeting is scheduled
- [ ] You know who will be on the call from their side

**Common Mistakes:**
- Leading with your product instead of their problem
- Sending one message and giving up (it often takes 3-4 touches)
- Using templates without personalization
- Asking for too much too soon ("Book a 45-min demo" in a cold email)

**Tools and Skills That Help:**
- `/cold-email` for drafting outreach
- [Messaging Structure](messaging-structure.md) for email structure
- [Positioning Levels](positioning-levels.md) for painkiller vs vitamin framing

### Stage 3: Discovery

**Objective:** Understand their world, their pain, and whether there's a real deal here. This is the most important stage.

**Key Activities:**
- Run a discovery call using SPIN questions
- Understand: their current process, what's broken, what it costs them, what solving it would mean
- Map the buying committee: who decides, who influences, who blocks
- Assess urgency: is there a deadline, trigger, or event driving this?
- Qualify with BANT: Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline
- Take detailed notes — especially their exact words about pain and impact

**Exit Criteria:**
- [ ] You can articulate their problem in their own words
- [ ] The prospect has quantified the impact of the problem (time, money, risk)
- [ ] You know who the decision-maker is (even if they're not in the room yet)
- [ ] You have enough context to build a relevant demo
- [ ] BANT score is at least 7/12 (Warm or Hot)

**Common Mistakes:**
- Treating discovery as a checkbox ("We did a discovery call") instead of going deep
- Asking only Situation questions (context without pain)
- Skipping Implication questions (the prospect admits problems but doesn't feel the cost)
- Pitching during discovery ("Oh, we solve that!" — too early)
- Not qualifying: spending 3 more calls on a prospect who can't buy

**Tools and Skills That Help:**
- [Pain Discovery](pain-discovery.md) (SPIN) for question structure
- [Discovery Questions](discovery-questions.md) (Mom Test) for honest signals
- [Prospect Qualification](prospect-qualification.md) (BANT) for qualification scoring
- `/analyse-call` to review your discovery call performance

### Stage 4: Demo

**Objective:** Show the solution mapped to their specific pain — not a generic product tour.

**Key Activities:**
- Build a tailored demo that mirrors their workflow and problems
- Structure: recap their pain (2 min) → show the solution (15 min) → discuss fit (10 min)
- Use their language and their examples, not generic ones
- Address objections that came up in discovery
- Get the decision-maker in the room if possible
- End with a clear next step: "Based on what you've seen, does a proof-of-concept make sense?"

**Exit Criteria:**
- [ ] The prospect confirms the solution addresses their problem
- [ ] They agree to a next step (POC, trial, proposal, or internal review)
- [ ] You know what the internal approval process looks like
- [ ] Any new stakeholders are identified and addressed

**Common Mistakes:**
- Showing every feature instead of only what solves their problem
- Running the same generic demo for every prospect
- Not recapping the pain at the start (so the demo feels disconnected from their world)
- Demoing to the wrong audience (ICs who can't decide, without the buyer present)
- No clear next step at the end ("Thanks, we'll be in touch" = death)

**Tools and Skills That Help:**
- Discovery notes from Stage 3 (their exact words about pain)
- [Positioning Levels](positioning-levels.md) for keeping demo in painkiller territory

### Stage 5: Proof

**Objective:** Demonstrate that your product works in their real environment with their real data. Remove the last barrier to buying.

**Key Activities:**
- Set up a POC, pilot, or free trial scoped to a specific problem
- Define success criteria upfront: "If we achieve X by [date], would you move forward?"
- Agree on timeline: start date, check-in points, decision date
- Provide hands-on support — don't leave them alone with a trial login
- Track usage and results to build the business case
- Regular check-ins: weekly for POC, mid-point for trial

**Exit Criteria:**
- [ ] Success criteria met (or clearly on track)
- [ ] Prospect confirms the product works for their use case
- [ ] Decision-maker has seen the results
- [ ] Commercial discussion is ready to happen (pricing, contract terms)
- [ ] No unresolved technical blockers

**Common Mistakes:**
- Unlimited POC with no defined scope or success criteria (it drags forever)
- No executive engagement during the proof — the IC loves it but can't get buy-in
- Treating the POC as "set and forget" — no check-ins, no support
- Not defining what happens after the POC: "If this works, what's the next step?"
- Scope creep: "Can we also test this other thing?" without extending timeline

**Tools and Skills That Help:**
- [Pipeline Scorecard](pipeline-scorecard.md) for tracking commitment during POC
- [Prospect Qualification](prospect-qualification.md) for re-qualifying after demo

### Stage 6: Close

**Objective:** Get the formal commitment — signed contract, purchase order, or verbal agreement backed by action.

**Key Activities:**
- Present a proposal or pricing based on what was proven in Stage 5
- Reference their quantified pain and POC results: "You said this costs you $300K/year. We proved we can cut that by 60%."
- Handle final objections: pricing, timing, internal politics
- Navigate procurement: legal review, security questionnaire, vendor approval
- Create urgency (genuine, not artificial): "If we start by [date], you'll have this in place before [their deadline]."
- Get the signature

**Exit Criteria:**
- [ ] Contract signed or PO issued
- [ ] Payment terms agreed
- [ ] Start date confirmed
- [ ] Key stakeholders on both sides identified for handoff

**Common Mistakes:**
- Discounting before they ask (negotiating against yourself)
- Not involving procurement early enough (adds weeks at the end)
- Losing momentum between POC and close (follow up within 48 hours of POC success)
- No executive sponsor: the champion tries to push it through alone and hits a wall
- Vague close: "They said yes on the call" — get it in writing

**Tools and Skills That Help:**
- POC results and prospect's own words from Stages 3-5
- [Pipeline Scorecard](pipeline-scorecard.md) for final commitment assessment

### Stage 7: Handoff

**Objective:** Transition the relationship from sales to customer success / delivery. Set the customer up to succeed.

**Key Activities:**
- Internal handoff: brief your CS/implementation team on everything you learned
- Customer kickoff: introduce the CS team, review the plan, set expectations
- Transfer all context: pain points, success criteria, stakeholder map, communication preferences
- Confirm onboarding timeline and milestones
- Set up the first check-in (30 days, 60 days, 90 days)
- Thank the champion — they stuck their neck out for you

**Exit Criteria:**
- [ ] CS / implementation team has full context
- [ ] Customer has met their post-sale point of contact
- [ ] Onboarding plan is documented and shared
- [ ] First 30/60/90 day milestones are defined
- [ ] Nothing falls through the cracks in the transition

**Common Mistakes:**
- No handoff at all: the founder stays as the primary contact forever (doesn't scale)
- Cold handoff: "Here's your new contact, bye!" — the customer feels abandoned
- Not transferring context: CS team asks the same questions the customer already answered
- Forgetting the champion: they fought internally for you. A thank-you goes a long way.
- No defined milestones post-sale: the customer drifts and churns at renewal

**Tools and Skills That Help:**
- Discovery and POC notes from Stages 3-5
- Internal CRM/Linear notes from throughout the deal

## How to Apply It

### Step 1: Map Your Current Deals

For every active deal, identify which stage it's in:

| Deal | Stage | Last Activity | Next Action | Stuck? |
|------|-------|--------------|-------------|--------|
| Acme Corp | Discovery | Call on March 10 | Follow up with case study | No |
| Beta Inc | Proof | POC started March 1 | Week 2 check-in | No |
| Gamma Ltd | Outreach | Email sent March 8 | Follow-up #2 | Maybe |

### Step 2: Check Exit Criteria

For each deal, check the exit criteria for its current stage. If any are unmet, that's your next action.

### Step 3: Move or Qualify Out

If a deal has been in the same stage for 2+ weeks with no movement, ask:
- Is the exit criteria achievable?
- Is the prospect taking action (not just talking)?
- Is this worth continued investment?

Some deals need to be moved backward (you skipped Discovery), and some need to be qualified out (the prospect isn't real).

### Step 4: Run the Checklist for Every New Deal

Don't skip stages. The most common failure mode is:

```
Research → Outreach → [Skip Discovery] → Demo → [Wonder why they ghosted]
```

The playbook exists to prevent this. Every deal gets every stage.

## Workshop: Build Yours

1. **Customize exit criteria.** The criteria above are generic. For each stage, what specifically must be true for *your* product?

2. **Define your typical timeline.** How long does each stage take for your deals? Where do deals tend to stall?

3. **Build your playbook library:**
   - Stage 2: 3 outreach message templates for different signals
   - Stage 3: Your SPIN question bank
   - Stage 4: Your standard demo flow (customizable per prospect)
   - Stage 5: Your POC scope template with success criteria

4. **Identify your most common failure point.** Is it Outreach (low response rates)? Discovery (not going deep enough)? Close (deals stalling after POC)? Focus your improvement there.

## Example: Full Deal Walkthrough

**Context:** You sell an automated code review tool. Here's a deal from prospect to close.

**Stage 1 — Research (Tuesday, 45 min):**
You find a Series B startup (120 people, 40 engineers) hiring 3 senior engineers. Their VP of Engineering posted on LinkedIn about "scaling engineering quality." Your ICP score: Core. Selling Window: Hurting (scaling pain + public acknowledgment).

**Stage 2 — Outreach (Tuesday-Friday):**
You send a cold email to the VP referencing the LinkedIn post and the hiring spree. Day 3 follow-up adds a case study from a similar company. Day 3 — VP replies: "Good timing, let's chat."

**Stage 3 — Discovery (Week 2, two calls):**
Call 1: SPIN discovery. Learn that code reviews take 2 days on average, they've had 3 production incidents from missed issues this quarter, and the VP is getting pressure from the CEO to ship faster. VP quantifies: "We're burning probably 30% of senior engineer time on reviews." BANT: 10/12.

Call 2: Deeper dive with a team lead. Map the workflow, understand tooling, identify integration requirements. Team lead becomes your champion.

**Stage 4 — Demo (Week 3):**
Tailored demo using their actual code review workflow. Show: AI reviews catch the types of issues that caused their production incidents. VP asks: "Can we try this on our monorepo?" (= asking for proof stage).

**Stage 5 — Proof (Weeks 4-6):**
2-week POC on their monorepo. Success criteria: "Reduce average review time by 40% and catch at least 2 issues that would have been missed." Week 1 check-in: on track. Week 2 results: review time down 55%, caught 5 issues that manual review missed.

**Stage 6 — Close (Week 7):**
VP presents results internally. You provide an ROI summary: "Based on the POC, this saves 15 senior engineer-hours per week. At your loaded cost, that's $180K/year. Annual license: $48K." Contract signed end of week.

**Stage 7 — Handoff (Week 8):**
Kickoff call with your CS lead, their VP, and team lead champion. Onboarding plan: full team rollout in 2 weeks. 30/60/90 day milestones defined. First check-in scheduled.

**Total time: 8 weeks.** Every stage had clear exit criteria met before advancing.

## Anti-Patterns

### Skipping Discovery
The most damaging mistake. You research, outreach, get a response, and immediately demo. Without Discovery, your demo is generic, your proposal is ungrounded, and the prospect ghosts you. Every deal gets a discovery call. No exceptions.

### Treating the playbook as a linear pipeline
Deals don't always move forward. A prospect at Demo might reveal new information that sends you back to Discovery. A POC might surface a new stakeholder who needs their own Discovery + Demo. Be willing to go backward to go forward.

### Rushing to Close after a successful Demo
"They loved the demo! Let me send a contract." Without Proof, the prospect can't justify the purchase internally. "I saw a cool demo" doesn't survive a CFO review. "We ran a POC and achieved [specific result]" does.

### No exit criteria
If you don't define what must be true before advancing, you advance on vibes. "It felt like a good call" is not an exit criterion. "The prospect quantified the pain at $200K/year and named the decision-maker" is.

### Founder stays through Handoff forever
As the founder, you're the best salesperson for your product right now. But if you're also the ongoing CS contact for every customer, you'll never have time for new deals. Build the handoff muscle early, even if it's handing off to yourself wearing a different hat.

### Perfectionism in Research
Some founders research for hours before reaching out. 30-60 minutes is plenty. You'll learn more in a 15-minute discovery call than in 3 hours of desk research. Good enough research + fast outreach beats perfect research + no outreach.
