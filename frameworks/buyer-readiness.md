# Buyer Readiness — Read Actions, Not Words

A matrix for diagnosing where a buyer actually is in their journey — by reading what they *invest* (time, effort, money, attention to quality) rather than what they *say* — and matching your response mode to that stage.

## What Is It?

Buyers move through four awareness stages on the way to a purchase: **Awareness → Interest → Consideration → Purchase**. The stage they're at determines what will move them forward and what will bounce off.

The problem: buyers routinely *say* things from a later stage than they're at. "We're very interested" is Interest-stage vocabulary — but if their behavior shows no invested time, no effort, no budget thinking, they're actually at Awareness. Selling to the words instead of the stage is how founders end up pitching proposals to window-shoppers.

Buyer readiness maps **behavioral signals** across the journey:

| Stage | Time signals | Effort signals | Money signals | Quality signals |
|-------|-------------|----------------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Awareness** | Skims content, seconds on page | Passive scrolling, no engagement | No budget thinking | Can't articulate the problem yet |
| **Interest** | Minutes exploring, revisits your profile/site | Brief replies, likes/comments, general questions | No cost awareness | Mentions broad problems without specifics |
| **Consideration** | Schedules calls, attends meetings, engages repeatedly | Shares internal details, explains challenges, invests prep time | Raises cost concerns, starts evaluating ROI, compares options | Defines the problem clearly, asks solution-specific questions |
| **Purchase** | Joins multiple calls, discusses timelines | Quotes internally, reviews proposals, negotiates scope | Allocates budget, discusses pricing, seeks approval | Requests proposals, defines scope, finalizes requirements |

Read the columns, not the transcript. Where the *behavior* clusters is where the buyer is.

## Why It Matters for 0-1 Sales

Early-stage founders systematically misread buyer stage in the optimistic direction — because they need the deal, and because buyers are polite. The costs:

- **Proposals sent to Interest-stage buyers** die in inboxes. The buyer wasn't evaluating; they were browsing.
- **Education delivered to Purchase-stage buyers** is condescending. They've already diagnosed themselves — they need pricing and scope, not a problem lecture.
- **Follow-ups calibrated to the wrong stage** (weekly "just checking in" on an Awareness buyer) burn goodwill without moving anyone.

The framework's second use is diagnostic in the other direction: two buyers can describe the *identical* pain in the same words, but one is at Consideration (mature operator who's evaluated options) and one is at Interest (frustrated but hasn't invested anything in solving it). **The framework tells you HOW to respond, not just WHAT to respond to.**

## The Framework

### Step 1: Place the buyer on the matrix

After any interaction, score each column: where does their time investment put them? Their effort? Their money signals? Their quality-of-problem-definition? The stage is where the majority of columns land — and mismatches are information (big talk + zero effort = polite Interest, not Consideration).

### Step 2: Identify their readiness type

Within a stage, buyers cluster into readiness types that need different responses:

| Readiness type | What it looks like | What they need | Response mode |
|----------------|--------------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Emotionally ready** | They *feel* the pain — scared, exhausted, overwhelmed. Vivid language about consequences. | Quantification | Don't educate. Put a number on what they're feeling so it translates into action. Make them say the number out loud. |
| **Intellectually ready** | Technically mature; understands the problem at a systems level. | Elevation | Skip the education — it's condescending. Raise the conversation from engineering symptoms to business consequences. |
| **Champion, can't close alone** | Personally convinced but needs internal buy-in. | Ammunition | Don't keep selling to them. Arm them: build the narrative for the decision-maker *they* need to convince. |
| **Conceptually aware, no urgency** | Sees the gap intellectually: "we should do something about that someday." | Urgency | Tie the abstract gap to specific, recent, costly consequences. Make "someday" feel like "now." |
| **Curious, not pained** | Interested in the idea; no operational pain to justify a purchase. | Qualification | Innovation curiosity ≠ buying intent. Disqualify gracefully or park as a future-stage / community relationship. |

### Step 3: Match your move to the diagnosis

The same product, the same pain sentence, five different correct responses. The readiness type — not the pain description — picks your play.

## How to Apply It

1. **Build your own signal table.** Take the four-stage matrix above and rewrite each cell for YOUR product and sales motion: what does Consideration-stage effort actually look like for you? (Sharing a staging environment? Introducing their team lead?)
2. **Score after every touchpoint.** 30 seconds: which column did this interaction produce evidence for? Did the stage change?
3. **Diagnose before you draft.** Before any proposal, demo, or follow-up, name the buyer's stage and readiness type. If you can't, your next move is discovery — not the proposal.
4. **Watch for stage-skipping.** A buyer whose deal stage (in your CRM) is ahead of their behavioral stage is a deal you've inflated. Pull your process back to where the buyer actually is.

## Workshop: Build Yours

1. Take your last 10 prospect conversations. For each, write down what the buyer SAID their status was.
2. Now score each on the four signal columns (time / effort / money / quality). Where were they actually?
3. Count the mismatches. (Most founders find 4-6 of 10 were a stage lower than the words suggested.)
4. For each mismatch: which readiness type were they? What response did you give vs. what the table prescribes?
5. Rewrite the four-stage matrix with YOUR product's specific signals — this becomes your scoring rubric for every future call.

## Example: Same Sentence, Two Buyers

**Product:** An automated API testing tool.

Two prospects say the identical sentence: *"Our test scripts keep breaking every release."*

**Buyer A** — a QA lead who has: attended two calls on time, shared their CI pipeline config, asked how pricing scales with test volume, and defined the problem precisely ("flaky selectors, roughly 20% of suite per release").
→ Columns: time ✓, effort ✓, money ✓, quality ✓. **Consideration/Purchase, intellectually ready.** Response: skip the education, elevate to business impact ("what does 20% suite maintenance cost you in coverage expansion?"), move to scope.

**Buyer B** — a founder who has: replied to one LinkedIn message, taken one call (rescheduled twice), no budget mention, problem described only as "testing is a mess."
→ Columns: time ~, effort ✗, money ✗, quality ✗. **Interest, conceptually aware.** Response: no proposal. Run consequence-based discovery to connect "a mess" to a number, and create urgency — or qualify out.

Same words. Different buyers. The matrix is what stops you from sending Buyer B the proposal that Buyer A earned.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **[Pipeline Scorecard](pipeline-scorecard.md)** — Same words-vs-actions philosophy, different altitude: the scorecard scores a *deal's* commitment; buyer readiness diagnoses a *person's* stage and tells you the response mode.
- **[Selling Window](selling-window.md)** — Selling Window reads the *organization's* pain stage; buyer readiness reads the *individual's* journey stage. An org in "Hurting" can still hand you a "curious, not pained" contact — you need both diagnoses.
- **[Positioning Levels](positioning-levels.md)** — Readiness tells you which positioning level is achievable. Emotionally-ready buyers can be taken to Painkiller; curious buyers will perceive you as a Vitamin no matter what you say.
- **[Consequence Questions](consequence-questions.md)** — The standard prescription for "emotionally ready" and "conceptually aware" types: quantify. Readiness diagnoses; consequence questions treat.
- **[Intent Signals](intent-signals.md)** — Intent signals find companies worth approaching; buyer readiness stages the individual once the conversation starts.

## Anti-Patterns

### Believing the words
"We're very interested" + zero invested effort = Awareness with good manners. Score the columns, not the sentiment.

### One response mode for everyone
Running the same pitch-demo-proposal sequence on every buyer means it's mismatched for 4 of the 5 readiness types. The diagnosis IS the sales skill.

### Educating the sophisticated
Teaching testing basics to a systems-thinking engineering leader tells them you weren't listening. Intellectually-ready buyers need elevation, not education.

### Pitching the champion harder
When your contact is convinced but the deal isn't moving, more enthusiasm at them is wasted. They don't need convincing — they need a CFO-ready narrative. Build it for them.

### Treating readiness as static
Buyers move — both directions. A "curious" buyer whose company hits a triggering incident becomes "emotionally ready" overnight; a Consideration buyer who goes silent has slipped back. Re-diagnose at every touchpoint, and re-score your [Revenue Visibility](revenue-visibility.md) sheet to match.
