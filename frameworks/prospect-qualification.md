# Prospect Qualification — BANT Framework

How to decide whether a prospect is worth your time — before you've invested hours in calls and demos.

## What Is It?

BANT is a qualification framework that evaluates prospects across four factors:

- **Budget** — Can they pay for a solution?
- **Authority** — Are you talking to someone who can make (or influence) the buying decision?
- **Need** — Do they have a real problem your product solves?
- **Timeline** — Is there urgency to solve this problem now?

Originally developed at IBM in the 1960s, BANT has become the most widely-used qualification framework in B2B sales. It's simple, memorable, and forces you to ask the questions early founders tend to skip.

## Why It Matters for 0-1 Sales

When you're early-stage, your biggest constraint is time. You can't afford to spend 3 weeks nurturing a prospect who:
- Has no budget and won't for 6 months
- Loves your product but can't approve a purchase
- Has a mild inconvenience, not an urgent problem
- Is "exploring options" with no deadline

BANT gives you a quick gut-check: is this deal real, or am I chasing a ghost?

**The hard truth:** Most early founders are so excited about interest that they skip qualification entirely. They treat every "that sounds cool" as a pipeline deal. BANT forces discipline.

## The Framework

### The Four Factors

| Factor | What You're Assessing | Key Questions |
|--------|----------------------|---------------|
| **Budget** | Can they fund this? Is there allocated spend? | "How are you handling this cost today?" / "Is there budget earmarked for this?" / "What's the cost of the current approach?" |
| **Authority** | Who decides? Who influences? Who blocks? | "Who else would need to weigh in?" / "How did you buy [similar tool] last time?" / "Walk me through your typical procurement process" |
| **Need** | Is the problem real, acknowledged, and painful? | "How are you solving this today?" / "What happens when [problem] occurs?" / "How much time/money does this cost you?" |
| **Timeline** | Is there urgency? What's driving the deadline? | "When do you need this solved by?" / "What happens if you don't solve this by [date]?" / "Is there an event driving this timeline?" |

### Scoring Each Factor

Rate each factor 1-3:

| Score | Budget | Authority | Need | Timeline |
|-------|--------|-----------|------|----------|
| **3** | Budget allocated or quantified willingness to pay | Decision-maker directly engaged, buying process clear | Problem acknowledged, impact quantified, actively seeking solution | Deadline identified, consequences of delay articulated |
| **2** | Budget exists in theory, not allocated; cost of problem understood | Champion identified but decision-maker not yet involved | Problem acknowledged but impact not quantified; "would be nice to fix" | General desire to solve "soon" but no hard deadline |
| **1** | No budget discussion, "we'd need to figure that out" | Talking to an evaluator with no buying authority | Mild interest, no clear problem articulated | "Sometime next year" or no urgency signals |

### Qualification Tiers

| Total Score | Tier | What It Means | Your Move |
|-------------|------|---------------|-----------|
| **10-12** | **Hot** | All factors strong. This deal is real. | Prioritize. Move to next stage fast. Don't let it cool. |
| **7-9** | **Warm** | Some factors strong, gaps to fill. | Worth pursuing. Focus next conversation on elevating the weak factors. |
| **4-6** | **Cool** | Multiple weak factors. Deal may not be real yet. | Nurture lightly. Don't invest heavy time until factors improve. |

## How to Apply It

### Step 1: Don't Lead with BANT Questions

BANT is your mental checklist, not your script. Don't open a discovery call with "What's your budget?" That kills conversations.

Instead, weave BANT into natural conversation:

- **Budget →** "How are you handling this cost today?" (This surfaces current spend without asking for a number.)
- **Authority →** "If this looked like a fit, who else would want to evaluate it?" (Maps the buying committee without asking "Are you the decision-maker?")
- **Need →** "Tell me about the last time [problem] happened. What was the impact?" (Validates pain with specifics.)
- **Timeline →** "What's driving the timing on this?" (Reveals whether there's real urgency or just curiosity.)

### Step 2: Score After the Call

After every discovery call, take 60 seconds to score BANT:

```
Prospect: [Company Name]
Date: [Call Date]

B: [1-3] — [One sentence justification]
A: [1-3] — [One sentence justification]
N: [1-3] — [One sentence justification]
T: [1-3] — [One sentence justification]
Total: [X/12]
Tier: [Hot/Warm/Cool]
```

### Step 3: Focus Your Next Interaction

Look at your lowest-scoring factor. That's what you need to elevate next.

- **Low Budget?** → Ask about cost of the current approach. Quantify the pain in dollars.
- **Low Authority?** → Ask to include the decision-maker in the next conversation. Offer a brief executive overview.
- **Low Need?** → Dig deeper on impact. Use implication questions (see [Pain Discovery](pain-discovery.md)).
- **Low Timeline?** → Look for triggering events. Ask what happens if they don't solve this by Q[X].

### Step 4: Don't Force It

If a prospect scores Cool (4-6) after two conversations with no improvement, move them to a nurture track. Don't keep pushing — you're spending time that could go to a Hot prospect.

## Workshop: Build Yours

Answer these for your product:

1. **Budget signals for your market:**
   - What does your target customer typically pay for similar tools?
   - What internal costs does your product replace? (headcount, tools, time)
   - What budget category would your product fall under?

2. **Authority patterns:**
   - Who typically discovers tools like yours? (IC, manager, director, VP?)
   - Who signs off on purchases at your price point?
   - Are there common blockers? (procurement, legal, security review)

3. **Need validation:**
   - What's the core problem you solve?
   - How do prospects currently handle it? (manual process, competitor, ignore it)
   - What makes the problem acute vs. tolerable?

4. **Timeline triggers for your market:**
   - What events create urgency? (funding round, new hire, compliance deadline, scaling pain)
   - What's a typical buying cycle length for your product category?
   - What makes prospects say "we need this NOW" vs. "maybe next quarter"?

## Example: B2B SaaS Evaluating a Mid-Market Prospect

**Context:** You sell an automated testing tool. A VP of Engineering at a 200-person company reached out after seeing your blog post.

**After the discovery call:**

```
Prospect: DataFlow Inc.
Date: March 15

B: 2 — They spend $180K/year on QA contractors. VP said "we know we need
       to invest in automation" but no specific budget allocated yet.
A: 3 — VP of Engineering is the buyer. CTO gave thumbs up to evaluate.
       VP bought their CI/CD tool last year, same process.
N: 3 — Release cycles taking 2 weeks instead of 2 days. CEO pressuring
       for faster shipping. 3 production bugs last month traced to
       insufficient test coverage. VP quantified at "hundreds of
       engineering hours per quarter."
T: 2 — Want to solve "this half." No hard deadline but Q3 planning
       starts in June and VP wants a recommendation by then.
Total: 10/12
Tier: Hot
```

**Analysis:** Strong deal. Budget is the only gap — they feel the pain and know it costs them, but haven't allocated specific dollars. Next conversation should quantify: "If you could cut that QA contractor spend by 60%, what would that free up?" This turns existing spend into budget justification.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **[Pipeline Scorecard](pipeline-scorecard.md)** — Use BANT scores to inform your scorecard. A prospect with Budget 3 and Authority 3 is showing Resource commitment. One with Need 3 and Timeline 3 shows Engagement and Problem Acknowledgment.
- **[Discovery Questions](discovery-questions.md)** — Mom Test techniques help you surface BANT signals without asking BANT questions directly. "Tell me about the last time..." is a Mom Test question that reveals Need.
- **[Pain Discovery](pain-discovery.md)** — SPIN questions are your primary tool for elevating a low Need score. Implication questions make the prospect feel the cost.
- **[Intent Signals](intent-signals.md)** — Intent signals are often proxies for BANT factors: hiring signals suggest Need and Timeline; budget announcements suggest Budget.
- **[Selling Window](selling-window.md)** — The Selling Window tells you if the org is ready to buy. BANT tells you if the individual prospect is qualified. Use both.
- **[Customer Profile](customer-profile.md)** — ICP fit is a pre-qualifier before BANT. If they're not in your ICP, don't bother scoring BANT.

## Anti-Patterns

### "Budget first" interrogation
Asking "What's your budget?" in the first call. This is a trust killer. Surface budget indirectly through cost-of-current-approach questions.

### Ignoring Authority
You have a champion who loves your product but can't buy it. You keep demoing and sending materials, hoping enthusiasm will bubble up. It won't. Ask: "Who would need to sign off?" — then get that person in the room.

### Confusing interest with Need
"They seemed really interested!" is not Need. Need means they have a problem, they know they have a problem, and the problem costs them something tangible. Interest without quantified pain is a vitamin, not a painkiller.

### Accepting "soon" as Timeline
"We'd like to do something about this soon" is not a timeline. A timeline has a date and a consequence: "We need this before our SOC 2 audit in September." Push for specifics.

### Over-qualifying
Don't turn every conversation into a qualification grilling session. BANT is a diagnostic you run in the background, not a checklist you march through out loud. If you find yourself asking all four factors mechanically, you're doing it wrong.

### Disqualifying too early
One low factor doesn't mean the deal is dead — it means you have work to do. A prospect with Need 3, Authority 3, Timeline 3, but Budget 1 might just need help building a business case. Don't walk away; help them justify the spend.
