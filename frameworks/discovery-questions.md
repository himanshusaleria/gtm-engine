# Discovery Questions — Ask questions even your mom can't lie about

**Attribution:** Based on Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*

## What Is It?

A set of rules for crafting customer conversations that produce honest, useful data instead of polite lies. The core principle: talk about **their life**, not **your idea**. If you pitch your solution and ask "would you use this?", everyone says yes to be nice. Instead, ask about their past behavior, current workflows, and real problems — things they can't fake.

## Why It Matters for 0-1 Sales

At the 0-1 stage, you don't have brand recognition, case studies, or a sales team. Every conversation with a potential customer is precious signal. But most founders waste these conversations by:

- Pitching too early and getting polite nods
- Asking hypothetical questions and getting hypothetical answers
- Hearing "that sounds great!" and mistaking it for demand

The Mom Test ensures you extract **real data** from every conversation, so you build conviction on facts, not compliments.

## The Framework

### Four Rules

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** You want to understand their world. The moment you describe your product, the conversation shifts from learning to selling.

2. **Ask about specifics, not generics.** "How do you usually handle X?" is generic. "Walk me through the last time X happened" is specific. Specifics reveal truth.

3. **Ask about the past, not the future.** "Would you pay for this?" is a future question — unreliable. "Have you tried to solve this before? What did you spend?" is a past question — reliable.

4. **Talk less, listen more.** If you're talking more than 30% of the time, you're pitching, not learning.

### Three Types of Bad Data

| Type | What It Sounds Like | Why It's Dangerous |
|------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Compliments** | "That's really cool!" / "I love the idea!" | Makes you feel validated without learning anything |
| **Fluff** | "We generally have issues with..." / "I always want better..." | Generic statements with no commitment behind them |
| **Ideas** | "You should add feature X!" / "What if it could also do Y?" | Feature requests disguised as demand — they're designing your product for you |

### Good Questions vs Bad Questions

**Bad questions** (reveal nothing):
- "Would you use a product that does X?"
- "How much would you pay for this?"
- "Do you think this is a good idea?"
- "Would this be useful for your team?"

**Good questions** (reveal truth):
- "How do you currently handle X?"
- "What happened the last time X went wrong?"
- "Walk me through your workflow when you need to do Y."
- "How much time does your team spend on X per week?"
- "Have you tried any solutions for this? What happened?"
- "Who else is involved when this decision gets made?"
- "What would happen if you didn't solve this for another 6 months?"

## How to Apply It

### Before the Conversation
1. Write down your 3 biggest assumptions about the customer's problem
2. Craft 5 questions that would **disprove** those assumptions (not confirm them)
3. Rewrite any question that mentions your product or solution

### During the Conversation
1. Start broad: "Tell me about how your team handles [problem area]"
2. Follow the energy — when they lean in or get animated, dig deeper
3. Anchor to specifics: "Can you give me a recent example?"
4. When you hear a complaint, quantify it: "How often? How much time? What's the cost?"
5. End with commitment: "Would you be open to [specific next step]?"

### After the Conversation
1. Write down the 3 most important things you learned
2. Separate **facts** (things they said about past behavior) from **opinions** (things they said about the future)
3. Update your assumptions — what changed?

## Workshop: Build Yours

1. **List your top 3 assumptions** about your target customer's problem
2. **Write 5 discovery questions** you'd ask in your next prospect call
3. **Check each question against the rules:**
   - Does it talk about their life or your idea?
   - Does it ask about specifics or generics?
   - Does it ask about the past or the future?
4. **Rewrite** any question that fails the check
5. **Add one "commitment question"** at the end (e.g., "Would you be willing to share your current workflow data with us?")

## Example

**Scenario:** You're building a B2B SaaS tool for managing customer onboarding.

**Bad discovery call:**
> "We've built an onboarding automation platform. It saves 10 hours per customer. Would your team find that useful?"
>
> Prospect: "Yeah, that sounds great. We definitely need better onboarding."

You learned nothing. They were being polite.

**Good discovery call:**
> "Walk me through what happens after a new customer signs their contract."
>
> Prospect: "Well, the CSM sends a welcome email, then we schedule a kickoff call. Usually takes 3-4 days to get on their calendar."
>
> "What happens between signing and the kickoff call?"
>
> Prospect: "Honestly, not much. The customer kind of sits there. We've had a few churn in the first month because they lost momentum."
>
> "How many customers churned in the first month last quarter?"
>
> Prospect: "Three. Out of maybe twelve new accounts."
>
> "Have you tried anything to fix that gap?"
>
> Prospect: "We tried a Notion checklist, but nobody maintained it. Our VP of CS has been talking about buying something, but it keeps getting deprioritized."

Now you have: a specific problem (3-4 day gap), quantified impact (25% first-month churn), a failed attempt (Notion), and a political signal (VP wants it but can't prioritize it).

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Customer Profile (ICP):** Discovery questions help you validate whether a prospect matches your ICP — or reveal new segments you hadn't considered
- **Intent Signals:** A great discovery call surfaces intent data you can't get from external signals alone
- **Messaging Structure:** The pain points you uncover become the "Issue" and "Impact" in your outreach messaging
- **Pipeline Scorecard:** The quality of answers in discovery directly feeds your commitment scoring

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | What Happens | Fix |
|-------------|-------------|-----|
| **The pitch disguised as a question** | "Don't you think automated onboarding would save you time?" — leads the witness | Remove your product from the question entirely |
| **The opinion collector** | Asking 10 people "would you use this?" and counting yeses | Ask about behavior, not opinions |
| **The feature request trap** | Prospect says "add Slack integration" and you write it on the roadmap | Ask "why?" three times — find the underlying need |
| **The happy ears problem** | Hearing "that's interesting" and marking it as positive signal | Only count specific commitments as positive: time, money, reputation |
| **Skipping the ugly truth** | Avoiding questions that might invalidate your idea | Those are the most important questions to ask |
| **The monologue** | Talking for 5 minutes about your vision before asking anything | Set a rule: no pitching in the first 15 minutes |
